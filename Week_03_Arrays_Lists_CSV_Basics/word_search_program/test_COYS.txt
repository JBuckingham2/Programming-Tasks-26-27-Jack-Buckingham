Glory, Glory: Why Tottenham Hotspur Are the Best Team in the Premier League

Ask a neutral to define "the best team" and they'll point at a league table or a trophy cabinet. That's the lazy answer. Tables only tell you who won the most matches across one season. Trophy cabinets reward whoever happened to have the richest owner in a given decade. If "best" means anything deeper, if it means the club that best embodies what football is for, then the answer sits in N17. Tottenham Hotspur are the best team in the Premier League, and the case rests on history, philosophy, infrastructure, drama, and a fanbase that has suffered more nobly than any other.

A History Built on Firsts
Start with the record books, because Spurs have more pioneering moments than clubs with twice the silverware.
In 1960–61, Bill Nicholson's side became the first team of the twentieth century to win the English league and FA Cup Double. That had been considered impossible in the modern game. Nobody had done it since the Victorian era, and the football establishment had more or less decided nobody would again. Spurs didn't just do it; they did it playing football that left opponents dizzy, winning their first eleven league games in a row.
Two years later, in 1963, Tottenham became the first British club to win a major European trophy, thrashing Atlético Madrid 5–1 in the Cup Winners' Cup final. Think about that. Before Liverpool's European dynasty, before Manchester United's 1968 triumph, before anyone in England knew what continental glory felt like, Spurs had already brought it home. In 1972 they won the first-ever UEFA Cup, and added a second in 1984.
Then, in May 2025, came Bilbao. A 1–0 win over Manchester United in the Europa League final ended seventeen years without a trophy and delivered Tottenham's first European title in four decades. It wasn't pretty. It didn't need to be. It was proof that this club, written off and mocked endlessly, still knows how to show up when it matters.
Being first matters. Anyone can follow a path once it's been cleared. Spurs have repeatedly been the ones clearing it.

The Philosophy: The Game Is About Glory
Every great club has a creed. Tottenham's is the best one in football.
Danny Blanchflower, captain of that Double-winning side, put it plainly: "The game is about glory." His point was that winning alone is a hollow ambition. Football should be about doing things in style, about attacking, about entertaining the people who pay to watch. The club's Latin motto, Audere est Facere, To Dare Is To Do, says the same thing. You don't sit back. You don't park the bus. You go for it.
That philosophy runs through the club's whole story. Glenn Hoddle, perhaps the most gifted English playmaker of his generation, spraying passes across White Hart Lane. Ossie Ardiles and Ricky Villa arriving from Argentina in 1978 and bringing a flair English football had never seen, culminating in Villa's slaloming FA Cup final winner in 1981, still one of the greatest goals ever scored at Wembley. Paul Gascoigne's free kick against Arsenal in the 1991 semi-final. Gareth Bale tearing Inter Milan's Maicon to pieces at the San Siro in 2010, scoring a second-half hat-trick in a game Spurs were losing 4–0, and then destroying Maicon again at White Hart Lane a fortnight later.
Other clubs win ugly and call it pragmatism. Spurs have always believed that how you play is part of who you are. You can argue that has cost them trophies. You cannot argue it hasn't made them the most watchable, most romantic club in the country.

The Best Stadium in the World
If you want a tangible, measurable argument, walk into the Tottenham Hotspur Stadium.
Opened in 2019, it holds over 62,000 people and is widely considered the finest football ground on the planet. The single-tier South Stand, holding around 17,500, is the largest of its kind in the UK and generates a wall of noise that visiting players openly talk about. It has a retractable pitch that slides away to reveal an NFL-standard artificial surface underneath, which is why the American football league chose it as its London home. It hosts boxing, concerts, and rugby. It has its own microbrewery.
Crucially, it was built without a sovereign wealth fund or an oligarch's chequebook. It was a long-term bet on the club's future, privately financed and delivered while the team kept competing at the top end of the league. Clubs with far greater spending power have spent years arguing over whether they can even afford to renovate their grounds. Spurs simply built the future.
A club's stadium is a statement of ambition. Tottenham's says: we intend to be here, at the top, for the next hundred years.

Legends Who Chose to Stay
Loyalty is a rare commodity in modern football. Spurs have produced players who embodied it.
Harry Kane came through the academy, was loaned out to Leyton Orient, Millwall, Norwich, and Leicester, and was dismissed as a one-season wonder after his breakthrough. He went on to become Tottenham's all-time record goalscorer, overtaking Jimmy Greaves, and England's record goalscorer too. He won three Premier League Golden Boots in a Tottenham shirt. Every one of those goals was scored by a local lad who grew up as a supporter.
Son Heung-min arrived from Bayer Leverkusen in 2015 and became one of the most beloved players in the league's history. He shared the Golden Boot in 2021–22 without scoring a single penalty, won a Puskás Award for a solo goal against Burnley in which he ran the length of the pitch, and formed with Kane the most prolific partnership in Premier League history. Crucially, he finished his Spurs career as a captain lifting a European trophy in Bilbao. That's the fairytale ending the club deserved, and that he deserved.
Go back further and you find Greaves, perhaps the purest finisher England has ever produced. Ledley King, whose knees barely held together but who was so good that Thierry Henry named him the best defender he'd faced, a man who once tracked back and dispossessed Arjen Robben without making a single foul. Steve Perryman, who made a club-record 866 appearances.
Great clubs are measured by the people who wear the shirt. Spurs have had some of the finest players and finest characters England has ever seen.

Nights Nobody Else Gets
Ask any football fan about the most dramatic nights in recent European history and Tottenham will feature heavily.
Amsterdam, 2019. Champions League semi-final, second leg. Spurs trail Ajax 3–0 on aggregate at half-time. Then Lucas Moura scores a hat-trick, the third in the 96th minute, to send Tottenham into their first-ever Champions League final. Mauricio Pochettino falling to his knees in tears is one of the iconic images of modern football.
That same campaign, in the quarter-final against Manchester City, Spurs went through on away goals after a frantic 4–3 defeat in which Raheem Sterling's late "winner" was ruled out by VAR. The tournament's greatest-ever tie, many argue, and Spurs came out the right side of it.
These are not the results of a mediocre club. These are the moments that make the sport worth watching, and Spurs produce them more often than anyone.

The Suffering Makes It Better
Now, the cynic will raise the obvious objection: Spurs haven't won the league since 1961. That's true. And it's exactly why they're the best.
Supporting a club that wins the league every other year is like eating dessert for every meal. It stops meaning anything. Tottenham fans have experienced the full range of human emotion: hope, collapse, the Lasagne Incident of 2006, finishing above Arsenal for the first time in a generation, losing finals, finding new and creative ways to fall short, and then, finally, Bilbao. When joy comes, it comes harder because it has been earned.
"Spursy" is a word rival fans use as a joke. But think about what it really describes: a club that keeps believing, keeps trying to play the right way, and keeps coming back every August with hope it has no rational reason to feel. That isn't weakness. That's devotion. A fanbase that stays loyal through decades without a title is worth more than a fanbase that turns up only when the trophies flow.

Conclusion
The Premier League has richer clubs. It has clubs with more recent titles. It has clubs with more expensive squads and more aggressive owners. But "best" was never just about money and medals.
Tottenham Hotspur were the first to do the modern Double. The first British club to conquer Europe. The first winners of the UEFA Cup. They have the finest stadium in the world, a footballing philosophy that puts glory above grinding results, a lineage of legends running from Greaves to Hoddle to Kane and Son, and a habit of producing nights that live forever in the sport's memory. And they have supporters who have stuck with them through everything.
Glory, glory, Tottenham Hotspur. To dare is to do. And nobody dares quite like Spurs.
